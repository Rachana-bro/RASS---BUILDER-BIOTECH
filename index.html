6. CLINICAL GENOMICS GUIDE & FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

How does the RASS-BUILDER Transformer AI eliminate noise without discarding real mutations? ↓
Standard legacy genomic pipelines rely on fixed static thresholds (e.g. discarding any read below Phred Q20 or depth below 20×). This heuristic causes high error rates in low-complexity regions and misses low-frequency somatic mutations (sub-clonal variants). RASS-BUILDER replaces static cutoffs with a transformer-based continuous neural mapping engine. By evaluating contextual flanking base-pair embeddings across both strands simultaneously, it separates genuine biological variant spikes from optical sequencer crosstalk and PCR stutter slippage.
What is the difference between a Germline variant and a Somatic variant? ↓
Germline variants are present in every cell of the body from conception and can be inherited from or passed down to biological children (typically ~50% VAF). In contrast, somatic mutations arise spontaneously in specific tissues (such as tumor cells) during a person's lifetime. Somatic mutations cannot be inherited by children, but they serve as critical targets for personalized cancer medicines like Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (e.g., Osimertinib for EGFR).
What does "ACMG Classification" mean (Pathogenic vs VUS vs Benign)? ↓
The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) established standardized criteria to classify DNA differences into 5 tiers:
  • Pathogenic / Likely Pathogenic: Sufficient scientific and clinical evidence proves this variant directly causes disease or increases disease risk. Actionable for medical intervention.
  • Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS): Existing evidence is insufficient or contradictory. Medical decisions should not be based solely on a VUS.
  • Benign / Likely Benign: Harmless variation found commonly in healthy populations with normal biological function.
What are the recommended next steps after receiving a Pathogenic result? ↓
A pathogenic result should always be discussed with a certified genetic counselor and the treating medical specialist (such as a medical oncologist). Recommended actions may include personalized targeted therapy (e.g., PARP inhibitors), enhanced disease surveillance (such as annual breast MRIs), preventative lifestyle and surgical consultations, and offering cascade predictive genetic testing to biological relatives.
